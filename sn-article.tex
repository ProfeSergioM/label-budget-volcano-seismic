\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Volcanic systems and monitoring network}\label{subsec:area}

The analysis uses the seismic catalog maintained by the Southern Andes
Volcanological Observatory (OVDAS), the agency responsible for permanent volcano
monitoring in southern Chile within Chile's National Geology and Mining Service
(SERNAGEOMIN) \cite{amigo_volcano_2021}. OVDAS operates dedicated local seismic
networks at the active volcanoes under surveillance and maintains continuous
event catalogs for each monitored system. Five of these systems were selected
according to three criteria: their catalogs hold large event volumes, their
combined catalogs include the event classes of interest, and they represent
distinct volcanic environments, so that the dataset spans heterogeneous
seismicity and recording conditions.

The third criterion defines the scientific contrast within the dataset.
Villarrica is an open-conduit system with an active lava lake and
near-continuous long-period (LP) seismicity
\cite{richardson_varying_2014}. Nevados de Chill\'an has sustained an eruption
with alternating effusive and explosive phases since 2016
\cite{cardona_volcanic_2021}. Copahue exhibits persistent hydrothermal
circulation \cite{casas_single-station_2024}. Laguna del Maule has undergone a
prolonged volcano-tectonic (VT) swarm associated with inflation of its
magmatic-hydrothermal system \cite{cardona_crustal_2018}. Hudson is a heavily
glaciated stratovolcano whose catalog includes brittle-fracture, fluid-resonance,
and surface-process seismicity \cite{agurto-detzel_seismicity_2014}. Together,
these systems offer contrasting volcanic settings, seismicity rates, and
catalog compositions.

The five systems span approximately 1100~km along the Southern Volcanic
Zone, from Laguna del Maule in the north to Hudson in the south, and each is
monitored by an independent local seismic network (Figure~\ref{fig:map}). The
waveforms used here come from one vertical-component channel per volcano,
sampled at 100~Hz (Table~\ref{tab:stations}). These records differ between
systems in instrument response, source--station distance, site conditions and
event population, and every quantity reported in this paper is accordingly
computed within a single system.

The interval covered by each system is the span over which its station
appears as the detecting station in the catalog, which is narrower than the
nominal 2013--2024 request at three of the five systems
(Table~\ref{tab:stations}). Laguna del Maule begins in April 2021, shortly
after station NI2Z entered operation on 30 March 2021. Copahue ends in August
2024 and Hudson in January 2024; both catalogs continue to December 2024
through other stations of their local networks, but the station used here
stops appearing as the detecting station on those dates. Hudson also begins
in March 2013 rather than January, because no continuous station record is
recoverable for its earlier entries. Nevados de Chill\'an and Villarrica span
the full January 2013--December 2024 interval.

\subsection{Nature of the catalog}\label{subsec:catalog}

The catalog results from analyst-based operational classification. For each
event, the analyst assigns an event class, identifies a detecting station,
selects an onset or phase-pick reference, and records a duration. The
reference time corresponds to the analyst-selected arrival at the detecting
station, and the duration is assigned during operational labeling as part of
the same entry.

Duration separates the classes as clearly as any waveform attribute, and it
varies between systems for a single class. Median analyst-assigned durations
run from 8.4~s for VT to 36.0~s for TO, with HB, VLP, LP and AV in between
(Table~\ref{tab:durations}). The same class also shifts between systems: LP
has a median duration of 11.0~s at Copahue and 29.7~s at Villarrica, and VT
of 8.0~s at Laguna del Maule and 17.9~s at Villarrica. A small number of
entries carry a non-positive duration, 1{,}457 events or 0.099\% of the
analyzed population.

Hypocentral solutions are available for a subset of the cataloged events.
Operational monitoring prioritizes timely event characterization, and event
classification consequently proceeds independently of a location estimate for
most entries. The events are therefore described here by waveform-derived
attributes.

\subsection{Event classes}\label{subsec:classes}

The OVDAS database contains a broader set of operational codes than the subset
used in the analysis, based on the classification proposed by
\cite{lahr_earthquake_1994} and \cite{chouet_volcano_2003}. The retained
population comprises six operational volcano-seismic classes: LP
(long-period), VT (volcano-tectonic), HB (hybrid), VLP (very-long-period;
coded LV in the database), AV (avalanche and rockfall), and TO (tornillo).
Between 2013 and 2024, these classes account for 63--97\% of catalog entries
across the five systems (Table~\ref{tab:rawcounts}).

Tremor (TR) is omitted because the code represents sustained or composite
seismic radiation rather than a consistently delimited discrete event. Sustained
tremor and dense sequences of LP events may generate comparable records, and
their operational separation can therefore depend on the temporal organization
of the signal. Restricting the analysis to discrete-event classes preserves a
common unit of observation across the dataset.

Three further database codes are excluded because they do not denote waveform
classes at all. EX marks an event with an observed surface manifestation, and
MF an event accompanied by an acoustic signal. Both are observational
attributes rather than mutually exclusive categories, since either can
accompany LP, HB, or tremor seismicity, and excluding them preserves a class
axis defined exclusively by waveform type. VA marks an event below the
amplitude threshold required for assignment to any specific waveform class,
and therefore denotes an operational detection category rather than a source
or signal type.

The six retained classes are assigned by analysts from waveform morphology and
spectral content and therefore represent operational signal categories rather
than direct determinations of source mechanisms
(Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}). The waveform-based descriptions below summarize
the interpretations commonly associated with these labels and are not treated
as independent measurements of source processes. LP events are characterized
by emergent onsets and narrow-band low-frequency energy, commonly associated
with resonance in fluid-filled cracks
\cite{chouet_long-period_1996}. VT events are characterized by impulsive
onsets and broadband energy extending toward higher frequencies, consistent
with signals commonly associated with brittle rock failure. HB events combine
an impulsive onset with a low-frequency coda
\cite{white_volcano-tectonic_2016}. VLP events are characterized
operationally by lower-frequency energy than LP events and may be
associated with slow fluid transport or conduit deformation
\cite{almendros_identifying_2002}. AV events are typically emergent,
broadband, and relatively long-duration surface signals
\cite{heck_localization_2019}. TO events exhibit slowly decaying,
quasi-monochromatic codas \cite{gomez_m_tornillo_1999}. The classes occupy
partially overlapping regions of waveform and spectral feature space; HB, in
particular, occupies a transitional region between LP and VT signals.


\subsection{Class composition}\label{subsec:composition}

The analyzed population is the part of the six-class catalog of
Table~\ref{tab:rawcounts} that was detected at the station of
Table~\ref{tab:stations} and whose record at that station is complete around
the event. Of the two conditions, the station is the more consequential at
every system except Villarrica, whose catalog is already dominated by its
station (Table~\ref{tab:filters}); together they retain 73--95\% of the
six-class catalog. The catalog also repeats a small number of entries, the
same event identifier at the same time: 928 at Nevados de Chill\'an, 48 at
Villarrica and 24 at Copahue, and none at Laguna del Maule or Hudson. Each is
counted once, and the analyzed population holds 1{,}477{,}410 distinct
events.

Class composition varies strongly among volcanic systems
(Table~\ref{tab:counts}). LP represents 99.3\% of the analyzed population at
Villarrica, whereas VT represents 99.5\% at Laguna del Maule. HB is the
dominant class at Copahue, accounting for 58.6\% of its analyzed population.
Nevados de Chill\'an contains five classes with substantial support, with LP
representing 87.2\% of its analyzed population. Hudson has the most even class
distribution among the five systems: VT, LP, and AV represent 44.0\%, 32.0\%,
and 22.5\% of the analyzed population, respectively, while HB contributes
1.4\%. Two cells of this matrix hold a single event: one HB at Villarrica and
one AV at Copahue. Both are reported as they appear in the catalog; a class
with one representative supports no estimate of within-class variability.

These proportions need not follow the dominant seismicity regime alone: they
may also reflect network sensitivity, station placement, recording conditions,
and operational labeling practices. The minority classes are distributed
unevenly for the same reason. Outside Copahue and Hudson, HB support is
sparse, with only isolated populations at Villarrica, Nevados de Chill\'an,
and Laguna del Maule. TO is represented only at Nevados de Chill\'an.
VLP representation is concentrated at Nevados de Chill\'an and Copahue, with
23 events at Villarrica; Laguna del Maule and Hudson contain no VLP events.
The AV label is operationally associated with signals attributed to unstable
slopes, glacier-related mass movement, or active crater-rim processes.
Hudson contains the largest relative AV population, 22.5\% of its analyzed
events, consistent with the range of surface and mass-movement processes
expected on a heavily glaciated edifice, followed by Nevados de Chill\'an at
6.1\%. Villarrica contributes 6{,}440 AV events in absolute terms, while its
much larger LP population limits the AV fraction to 0.7\%; AV has limited
representation at Copahue and Laguna del Maule.

Class composition also varies through time. Event rates track the temporal
development of each volcanic system, so class proportions depend on the

excursions.


\subsection{External catalogs}\label{subsec:external}

Four catalogs compiled by other agencies provide labeled records from
volcanoes and observatories outside the OVDAS network. They serve as an
external validation of the descriptors and are not part of the analyzed
population of Table~\ref{tab:counts}. This external validation is the second
stage of the programme opened in \cite{morales_recurrence_2026}, which
established the recurrence and spectral descriptors on the five OVDAS
systems alone; here the same descriptors are measured on records labeled
under other operational practices. Table~\ref{tab:external} summarizes the
four catalogs.

The Deception Island dataset \cite{cortes_standardization_2019}, deposited
in \cite{cortes_vsr_2018}, comprises two independent deployments on the same
Antarctic volcano, in 1995 and 2009, with different stations and
instruments. Records are sampled at 50~Hz, and its label file delimits every
event by a start and an end. A single record holds several labeled segments,
typical of the swarms the volcano produces, and every LP and VT segment of
both deployments is used.

VCSEIS \cite{zhong_deep-learning-based_2024}, distributed in SeisBench format
\cite{woollam_seisbench_2022}, covers four volcanic regions catalogued by the
Alaska Volcano Observatory, the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, the Northern
California Earthquake Data Center and the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network
(Cascade Range). Records are sampled at 100~Hz, and each trace carries P and S
arrival samples. Class labels come from the catalog field that distinguishes
long-period from local events; traces carrying any other code are not used.
The catalog distributes one trace per station, so an earthquake recorded at
several stations appears in several traces. A random draw of 500 traces per
region and class is used.

The VOISS-Net training set \cite{fee_generalized_2025} labels 120~s windows
of continuous data from volcanoes in Alaska and Hawai`i, one label per window
and per network. Its Earthquake and Long Period labels correspond to VT and
LP. The two classes come from different volcanoes: Earthquake from
K\={\i}lauea, Redoubt and Tanaga, Long Period from Veniaminof, Semisopochnoi
and Pavlof, so each of these six systems contributes a single class.
Waveforms are retrieved from the EarthScope data center at the vertical
channel of one station per volcano, sampled at 40, 50 or 100~Hz depending on
the station, with 60~s before and 120~s after each labeled window.

The Servicio Geol\'ogico Colombiano (SGC) provided 500 events per class from
Nevado del Ruiz, labeled LP, VT and HB by its analysts and extracted from the
waveform archive as one file per event holding the whole network, sampled at
100~Hz. The files carry no catalog onset or duration. The SGC designates
Olleta as the reference station for the volcano and Bis as its backup.

Labels for tremor, explosions, noise, regional events and other codes have
no counterpart among the six OVDAS classes and are not used. The external
catalogs therefore contribute LP and VT, and HB at Nevado del Ruiz.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methods}

The pipeline converts each event of the catalogs described above into
sixteen descriptors computed on two windows of its waveform: a fixed-length
window that opens at the onset, and a window that follows the event to the
end of its coda. The steps are described in the order in which they are
applied, from the choice of a station to the descriptors themselves, and the
value of every parameter is given where the step that uses it is defined.

\subsection{Station selection}\label{subsec:stationselection}

At each OVDAS system, the analysis draws on a single station: the one that
combines the largest number of catalogued detections with uninterrupted
operation (Table~\ref{tab:stations}). Restricting the analysis to one station
per system keeps a single instrument response and a single source--station
geometry within each class. The same rule holds at the external catalogs,
where each region, volcano or station is treated as a system of its own.

\subsection{Fixed-length window}\label{subsec:conditioning}

Every event is first represented by a window of 10~s, 1{,}000 samples at
100~Hz, that opens at the analyst-assigned onset, so that every sample
entering a descriptor belongs to the event. The window is extracted from a
longer padded segment, from 7~s before the onset to 13~s after it, which is
detrended (linear) and band-pass filtered between 0.5 and 10~Hz with a
zero-phase fourth-order Butterworth filter before being trimmed. The padding
leaves a 3~s margin beyond each end of the intervals the window uses, which
holds the filter's edge transient clear of them. The amplitude response of
the filter is 50.0\% at 0.5~Hz, 83.8\% at 0.6~Hz, 95.7\% at 0.7~Hz and 99.9\%
from 1.0~Hz, and at the upper end 100.0\% at 2.5 and 4.5~Hz, 98.6\% at
6.5~Hz, 89.4\% at 8~Hz and 50.0\% at 10~Hz.

Because the catalog carries no multi-station origin time, the padded segment
must come from a single, gap-free trace at the selected station spanning the
full interval. An event whose record leaves this interval incomplete, through
a data gap, an interruption, or a date before the station was installed, does
not enter the analyzed population; this is the second condition of
Table~\ref{tab:filters}. An event whose filtered window has zero variance has
no defined z-score and is excluded as well.

A signal-to-noise ratio is computed from the same padded segment, over a
noise window of 2~s, from 4 to 2~s before the onset, and the signal window:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR_{dB}} = 20\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{RMS}(\text{signal window})}{\mathrm{RMS}(\text{noise window})}.
\end{equation}
The 2~s separation between the noise window and the onset keeps an emergent
arrival out of the noise measurement. Both windows entering the ratio are
measured on the segment band-passed between 0.5 and 10~Hz. At lower
frequencies the record at these stations is dominated by the secondary
oceanic microseism, whose amplitude follows ocean swell and station distance
to the coast, so measuring the ratio over this band makes it a statement
about recording quality where the events of Table~\ref{tab:counts} radiate.
VLP events, whose energy extends below the band, are the one class the ratio
understates. The ratio characterizes the recording of each event, which
separates a class that stands out because of where it is recorded from one
that stands out because its source is distinctive. The signal window is
z-scored (zero mean, unit variance) before any descriptor is computed from
it, so that every descriptor is computed on a dimensionless signal.
Figure~\ref{fig:window} sets out these intervals on one event.

\subsection{Coda-length window}\label{subsec:codawindow}

The fixed-length window spans the full event for only a part of the
catalog. Set against the analyst-assigned durations, it covers the whole
event for 11.9\% of the analyzed population, with sharp differences between
systems: 71.8\% at Copahue, 67.5\% at Laguna del Maule, 34.6\% at Hudson,
4.4\% at Nevados de Chill\'an and 0.2\% at Villarrica. For the rest it
captures the onset and the early coda. A second window therefore follows each
event to the end of its coda, so that its length matches the duration of the
event. The two windows describe different parts of the record: the
fixed-length window compares events over an identical interval, dominated by
the onset and the early coda, while the coda-length window describes each
event as a whole. The sixteen descriptors are computed on both windows for
the same events, which shows which properties of a class are carried by its
onset and which by its full coda.

At the OVDAS systems the end of the coda is given by the analyst-assigned
duration. The window opens at the onset and spans that duration, from a
minimum of 5~s, below which a record holds too few samples for the
recurrence descriptors, to a maximum of 100~s, 10{,}000 samples, beyond which
a long coda is measured on its first 100~s. Events shorter than 5~s,
including the entries of non-positive duration, are represented by the
fixed-length window alone. The window is extracted from a padded segment
from 10~s before the onset to 3~s after the end of the coda, detrended and
band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 10~Hz with a zero-phase fourth-order
Butterworth filter, whose amplitude response is 50.0\% at 0.1~Hz, 96.6\% at
0.15~Hz and 99.7\% from 0.2~Hz, and at the upper end 99.9\% at 4.5~Hz,
97.6\% at 6.5~Hz, 87.3\% at 8~Hz and 50.0\% at 10~Hz. Its noise window spans
5~s, from 7 to 2~s before the onset, and the signal-to-noise ratio is
measured, as for the fixed-length window, between 0.5 and 10~Hz. Figure~\ref{fig:codawindow} sets the two windows side
by side on one event.

\subsection{Manual segmentation of the external records}\label{subsec:manualcut}

The external catalogs of Table~\ref{tab:external} supply labels, but none of
them supplies the three references the windows require, an onset, the end of
the coda and a pre-event noise window, in the form the OVDAS catalog does.
The three are therefore set by hand for every external event, by a single
analyst, following one protocol across the four catalogs.

Each record is displayed as its trace, band-passed between 0.5 and 10~Hz
with a zero-phase fourth-order Butterworth filter, and its spectrogram up to
25~Hz, together with the reference the catalog supplies: the labeled segment
at Deception Island, the P and S arrivals in VCSEIS, the 120~s labeled window
in VOISS-Net. The display starts from an automatic proposal, which the
analyst accepts or corrects for every event. The proposed onset is the
catalog arrival or segment start where one exists, and otherwise the first
trigger, within the labeled window, of a recursive STA/LTA detector with
windows of 0.5 and 5~s and trigger and release thresholds of 3 and 1, run on
the record band-passed between 1 and 10~Hz. The proposed end of the coda is
the catalog segment end where one exists, and otherwise the point where a
smoothed envelope of the record, band-passed between 0.5 and 10~Hz, falls
below 1.2 times its pre-event level and stays below it for 2~s. The proposed
noise window spans 12 to 2~s before the onset. The analyst then sets the
onset at the first arrival of the labeled event, the end of the coda where
the signal returns to the pre-event level, and the noise window immediately
before the onset, free of other events where the record allows it.

Figure~\ref{fig:manualcut} shows the three references set on one
event of each catalog, against the catalog reference and the automatic
proposal.

An event is kept when its onset falls within the reference the catalog
supplies and its coda can be delimited within the record. It is discarded
when no event is visible within that reference or when it lasts less than
5~s, and it does not enter a window whose padded segment its record does not
hold. At Nevado del Ruiz the events are reviewed at Olleta, and an event
that is not visible there is reviewed at Bis, following the SGC; the station
on which an event is cut is recorded with it, and Olleta and Bis are treated
as two systems, since a station change alters the record of a class. In
VOISS-Net the windows are reviewed in random order within each volcano and
class. The analyst may also revise a label; an event relabeled as a tornillo
(TO) is kept within LP, the class it was catalogued as, and flagged.

An event that enters the review more than once is kept once: an earthquake
recorded at several VCSEIS stations, identified by its catalog source
identifier, and an event spanning two consecutive VOISS-Net windows or two
consecutive SGC files, identified by onsets less than 3~s apart at the same
system. Of each group, the copy whose onset falls within its labeled
reference and has the highest signal-to-noise ratio is kept.

External records sampled at 50 or 40~Hz are brought to 100~Hz by polyphase
resampling, with rational factors of 2 and 5/2, before any filtering; records
whose nominal rate is 100~Hz enter at that rate. From that point on, both
windows and all sixteen descriptors are computed with the same code and
parameters as at the OVDAS systems, with the manually set onset and end of
coda in place of the catalog references.

\subsection{Class support criterion}\label{subsec:classsupport}

A class enters the per-class analyses of a volcanic system only if that
volcano-class combination holds at least 100 events in the analyzed
population of Table~\ref{tab:counts}. From that count a class carries enough
events to represent the range it spans at that system, and a measured
property reflects the class rather than the particular events that entered
it. A combination below the threshold remains part of the analyzed
population (Table~\ref{tab:counts}, Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries}). The
threshold is a convention fixed in advance and applied uniformly to every
volcano-class combination, which keeps the set of classes a system
contributes independent of any later result. Eighteen of the thirty
combinations of Table~\ref{tab:counts} clear it: five at Nevados de
Chill\'an, four at Copahue and Hudson, three at Villarrica and two at Laguna
del Maule. Every system therefore contributes at least two classes, and no
class is admitted at every system. The two single-event cells, one HB at
Villarrica and one AV at Copahue, fall far below it.

\subsection{RQA and spectral feature extraction}\label{subsec:features}

Sixteen descriptors are computed on each window: thirteen time-domain
recurrence descriptors and three spectral descriptors. Both act on the same
signal window, and no descriptor combines information from more than one
event. The same values apply to both windows.

The thirteen recurrence descriptors, $DET$, $L$, $L_{max}$, $DIV$,
$L_{entr}$, $LAM$, $TT$, $V_{max}$, $V_{entr}$, $W$, $W_{max}$, $W_{div}$, and
$W_{entr}$, are computed by recurrence quantification analysis (RQA)
\cite{marwan_recurrence_2007} on the z-scored window, with an embedding
dimension of 2, a time delay of 3 samples, and a Theiler correction of 1,
which excludes immediate temporal neighbors from the neighborhood count.
Diagonal, vertical, and white vertical line structures are counted from a
minimum length of 2 samples alike (Figure~\ref{fig:rqa}). The neighborhood
radius $\varepsilon$ is selected per event from the grid
$\{0.1, 0.2, 0.4\}$: the first radius whose recurrence rate ($RR$) falls
within $[0.01, 0.10]$ is accepted, and if none does, the radius whose $RR$ is
closest to the midpoint of the narrower band $[0.01, 0.05]$ is used instead.
$DIV$ and $W_{div}$, both defined as the reciprocal of a longest-line length,
are set to missing rather than infinite when that length is zero; if every
radius in the grid fails to produce a usable recurrence plot, all thirteen
descriptors, together with $\varepsilon$ and $RR$, are left missing for that
event. Frequency information enters through the three spectral scalars
defined next.

The three spectral descriptors, $f_{\text{peak}}$, $SR$, and $FI$, are
computed from the real FFT of the filtered, non-z-scored signal window, over
the band from 1.0 to 8.0~Hz. That band lies within the flat part of the
filter response, so the descriptors reflect the signal rather than the
filter. $f_{\text{peak}}$ is the frequency of maximum spectral amplitude in
that band, and $SR$ is the ratio of mean to maximum spectral amplitude over
the same band, following \cite{cui_subdivision_2021}, a measure of spectral
concentration, low when energy is confined to a narrow band and approaching
1 for a flat, broadband spectrum. $FI$ contrasts two narrower bands,
following \cite{cui_subdivision_2021} and \cite{buurman_seismic_2010}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FI} = \log_{10}\frac{\overline{A}(4.5\text{--}8.0~\mathrm{Hz})}{\overline{A}(1.0\text{--}2.5~\mathrm{Hz})},
\end{equation}
where $\overline{A}$ denotes mean spectral amplitude over the stated band.
The interval from 2.5 to 4.5~Hz is left out of both, so that the two bands
do not adjoin.

The VLP class sits outside the range these three descriptors resolve. A very
long period event radiates, by the definition that separates the class from
LP, at frequencies lower than the classes it is contrasted against, and its
dominant frequency lies below the evaluation band. For a VLP event
$f_{\text{peak}}$ therefore reports the strongest spectral component the band
admits, and the class characteristic that most directly distinguishes it, the
frequency at which it radiates most energy, is a quantity this measurement
places a lower bound on. The three descriptors stay comparable across events,
since one band applies to all of them; the reading of $f_{\text{peak}}$ as a
physical dominant frequency holds for the other five classes.

of each class, and Figure~\ref{fig:rqa} the recurrence plot of the same
events.
